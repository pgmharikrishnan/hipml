Explain, in continuous prose and without code, why a write-ahead log lets a
database recover after a crash even though the data pages on disk may be only
partially written. Your answer should cover the ordering guarantee between the
log and the data files, what a checkpoint does, how redo and undo differ, and
why torn pages are handled separately from committed transactions.

Use this concrete case as the thread of the explanation:

A banking database has two accounts, A and B, and a transfer of 100 units is
in flight. The transaction has already appended a log record that says "debit
A by 100" and "credit B by 100", and the log record is flushed to stable
storage. The corresponding data page for account A has been written to disk,
but the page for account B has not. Then the machine loses power while a third
page, belonging to an index, is halfway through a 16 KB write. When the
machine comes back, the storage stack reports that the index page has a bad
checksum, the account A page is intact, and the account B page still contains
its old value.

Walk through what the recovery code must do in that state. Distinguish what is
guaranteed by the log flush from what is merely likely, explain why the index
page is not repaired by replaying the transaction log, and describe the role
of checkpoints in bounding how much log has to be read. Mention the cost of
forcing the log on every commit, the reason group commit exists, and the trade
off between checkpoint frequency and recovery time. Aim for about 500 words.
Do not assume the reader has implemented a storage engine, but do assume they
understand transactions and durable writes.
