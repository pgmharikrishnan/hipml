The water cycle describes the continuous movement of water between the ocean, the atmosphere, and the land. Energy from the sun drives evaporation from water and transpiration from plants, lifting water vapour upward. As the vapour rises it cools, and condensation forms tiny droplets that gather into clouds. Winds carry those clouds across the planet, and when the droplets grow heavy enough they fall as rain, snow, or hail. Some of the precipitation lands on rivers and lakes and returns to the sea. Some soaks into the soil, where gravity pulls it downward through pores and cracks until it reaches the water table. That groundwater can travel for decades through rock before emerging at a spring or being drawn up by a well. Plants take up shallow groundwater and release it through their leaves. Snow and ice store water for months, releasing it slowly during the melt season. Runoff carves channels, carries sediment, and eventually rejoins the ocean, where the cycle begins. Human use alters the timing and the path: reservoirs hold back floods, irrigation moves water onto fields, and cities seal the ground with pavement. Understanding each step matters because the same molecule may pass through all of them.
